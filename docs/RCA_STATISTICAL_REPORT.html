Part A · The plain-language guide

Part A explains the data, what the method does, what a fault looks like, and what every test and number means, without equations. Part B, further down, is the full technical report with the code and every statistic.

A1. The problem, in one picture

A web shop is not one program. It is a group of small programs, called services, that call each other. The cart asks the feature-flag service, the checkout asks the cart, payment and shipping, and so on.

When one service breaks, the services that depend on it start failing too. On a dashboard, many things turn red at once. The hard question is which one broke first: the root cause. A second question is who will be pulled in next: the cascade.

This method answers both with no labels and no training: it only compares each service with its own recent normal behaviour.

An everyday comparison. In a block of flats the water pressure drops in ten flats at once. The ten flats are victims; the broken pump in the basement is the root cause. You find the pump by asking who the others depend on, and who shows a problem of its own rather than one inherited from below.

A3. The data: which system, which services

Dataset. The OpenTelemetry Demo, "Astronomy Shop": a realistic online shop made of 17 services. It is widely used to test monitoring tools. We recorded 76 minutes of healthy traffic with no faults, from its own tracing (Jaeger).

What we measure. One measurement row is taken every 15 seconds (a "tick"), giving 307 ticks. Each row holds, per service:

  • p95 latency: how long the slowest 5% of requests took.
  • error share: the fraction of requests that failed.
  • request count: how many requests came in during the tick.
  • self time: time spent inside the service itself, not waiting for others.
  • up / down: whether the service is running.

Which services get faults. Low-traffic services report nothing in many ticks, because no request touched them. Faults are injected only into services that report in at least 40% of ticks: 11 of 17 (ad, cart, currency, flagd, frontend, frontend-proxy, frontend-web, image-provider, load-generator, product-catalog, recommendation).

A4. What "baseline" means

The method never uses a fixed "normal" number. For every service and every signal, at every tick, it looks at the last 60 ticks (15 minutes) before that moment. From those it takes the typical value (the median) and the typical spread. That is the baseline.

Three rules keep the baseline honest:

  • Only the past is used. The method never peeks at the future.
  • Ticks that were already alarming are left out of the baseline. Otherwise a long fault would slowly start to look "normal".
  • A service is only judged after it has at least 12 usable past ticks. Here the frontend is first judged at tick 14, 4 minutes in.
What you are looking at.

The blue line jumps around a lot, even though nothing is wrong. That is normal: real traffic is noisy. Gaps in the line are ticks where the frontend had no requests to measure.

The pink baseline follows the general level, and drops after the half-way point when the traffic pattern changed. It moves on its own; nobody told it.

The dashed line is the edge of "normal wobble". Only when the blue line goes above it, and stays there, would the method raise an alarm.

The lesson: "normal" is a moving range, not a fixed number. That is why a fixed rule like "alarm if latency > 200 ms" would either miss faults or alarm constantly.

The blue line is the real latency of the frontend (milliseconds, log scale). The pink line is its baseline, which moves with the data. Being above the dashed line, three spreads above typical, is unusual; staying there is what raises an alarm.

A5. How the test is built, and what happens when a fault is injected

Stretching the recording. The 77 healthy minutes are played forwards, then backwards, then forwards again, eight times. That gives a continuous 10-hour stretch of realistic healthy traffic without jumps.

Injecting faults. After the first 40 minutes, a fault is injected every 10–15 minutes, lasting 2–4 minutes, into a randomly chosen eligible service. Every injection is written down in a ledger (which service, what kind, from when to when). The ledger is the answer key. The method never sees it; it is used only to mark the method's answers right or wrong.

A7. What each statistical test is for, in plain words

A result like "70% vs 42%" could be luck. Statistics answer: if both methods were really equally good, how surprising would this difference be?

p-value. The chance of seeing a difference at least this big if there were really no difference. Below 0.05 is the usual "unlikely to be luck" line; "<0.001" means far below it.

95% confidence interval (written [66%, 73%]). The range in which the true accuracy most plausibly lies, given how much the result varies.

Effect size. How big the difference is, not just whether it is real. With hundreds of faults, even a tiny difference can be "significant", so we report both.

2. How it works

1 Prepare→2 Score→3 Threshold + persistence→4 Common-mode removal→5 Attribute (root cause)→6 Risk (cascade)

No labels and no training. Everything is judged against each service's own recent healthy past, and only data available at that moment is used, so there is no look-ahead.

1 · Prepare (core/prepare.py)

Raw telemetry rows become a cube X[tick, service, feature]. Cumulative counters (network, disk) become per-second rates, and a counter going down after a restart becomes unknown, not a spike. The first row after a collection gap is dropped. A container that vanishes while others still report gets container_up = 0, so a crash reads as "broken", not as "no data".

2 · Score (core/score.py)

z[t,s,f] = d_f · ( x[t,s,f] − median(B) ) / max( 1.4826·MAD(B), q90(|B−median|)/1.645, binomial spread, floor_f ),  clipped at 0

Baseline window. B is the last window ticks before t (after a short guard), using only samples that were not themselves anomalous. This self-healing baseline means a long fault never becomes "normal".

Robust, one-sided score. d_f is the direction that means trouble, so a latency drop is not a fault. Positive quantities are scored on a log scale. Median and MAD replace mean and std, so one spike cannot inflate the spread. The 90% tail term handles mostly-zero series. Error rates also get the binomial spread √(p(1−p)/n): 1 error in 4 requests is weak evidence, 40 in 100 is strong.

Silence signal. For a service that normally reports, this is a binomial z of how far its recent activity fell below its usual rate. It is how a frozen service becomes visible at all.

Per-series noise normalisation. A series that is jumpy even when healthy is shrunk so that its healthy extreme sits at z = 3. Quiet series are never amplified. One threshold is then fair to every service.

3 · Threshold + persistence (core/threshold.py)

A service is flagged when its score is above τ for persistence consecutive ticks. Ticks with no data neither extend nor break a streak. τ is not guessed. It is the smallest value whose alarm episodes on healthy history stay under a false-alarm budget, by default 2 per hour.

4 · Common-mode removal (core/score.py)

If more than half the services move together on one symptom feature, such as a traffic burst, the shared part (the median score) is subtracted. One burst is not 20 faults.

5 · Attribute: source or victim? (core/rca.py)

score(s) = max( own(s), max(0, symptoms(s) − γ · maxcallee c evidence(c)) ) + 100 · [container stopped]

Own evidence is CPU, memory, self time, container_up and silence. Symptoms are errors, end-to-end latency, traffic and log errors, which can be inherited from a broken dependency. A broken callee explains away its callers' symptoms.

A service with no data whose callers have unexplained symptoms is inferred as the likely root. "Quiet" is inherited downstream: if my callers went quiet, my silence is theirs.

6 · Risk: who is next? (core/cascade.py)

risk(v) = 1 − Πflagged callees u of v ( 1 − severity(u) · p(u→v) ),   severity = min(1, zu / 2τ)

Edge probabilities. p(u→v) starts at the structural prior 0.5. It can instead be learned without labels across incidents: a trial is an alarm onset at a callee, and a success is the caller alarming within 4 ticks, with an empirical-Bayes prior.

Path probabilities. Multi-hop propagation uses the best (max-product) path within 3 hops.

def sorted_q(A, n, q):
    """q-quantile along axis 0 of an array already sorted along axis 0 (NaN sort last); n = valid count per column."""
    idx = np.clip(np.floor(q * (np.maximum(n, 1) - 1)).astype(int), 0, A.shape[0] - 1)
    out = np.take_along_axis(A, idx[None], axis=0)[0]
    return np.where(n > 0, out, np.nan)

    direction = np.array([SPEC[f][0] for f in p.features], float)

    for j, f in enumerate(p.features):

            X[:, :, j] = np.log(np.maximum(X[:, :, j], 0) + UNIT.get(f, 1.0))

        Xs = X.copy()                                                   # a sustained shift survives, a one-tick spike does not
        for j in range(F):
            if p.features[j] == "container_up":
                continue
            df_ = pd.DataFrame(X[:, :, j])
            Xs[:, :, j] = np.where(np.isnan(X[:, :, j]), np.nan, df_.rolling(smooth, min_periods=1).median().to_numpy())
        X = Xs
    k_err = p.features.index("trace_errors") if "trace_errors" in p.features else None
    k_rate = p.features.index("span_rate") if "span_rate" in p.features else None
    Z = np.zeros((T, S, F)); MED = np.full((T, S, F), np.nan); SC = np.full((T, S, F), np.nan)
    J = np.zeros((T, S, F), bool)
    usable = ~np.isnan(X)
    with warnings.catch_warnings():
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", RuntimeWarning)
        for t in range(T):
            lo, hi = max(0, t - window - guard), max(0, t - guard)
            if hi - lo < 5:
                continue
            B = np.where(usable[lo:hi], X[lo:hi], np.nan)              # only past samples that were normal
            n = np.sum(~np.isnan(B), axis=0)
            med = sorted_q(np.sort(B, axis=0), n, 0.5)                  # median of the baseline (NaN-aware, one sort)
            dev = np.sort(np.abs(B - med), axis=0)
            spread = np.maximum(sorted_q(dev, n, 0.5) * 1.4826, floor)  # 1.4826 x MAD = std for normal data
            if tail_q:                                                  # heavy tails / mostly-zero series: MAD collapses, so also
                spread = np.maximum(spread, sorted_q(dev, n, tail_q) / 1.645)   # respect the 90% deviation
            if binomial and k_err is not None and k_rate is not None:  # error RATE: 1 error in 4 requests is weak evidence

            if self_heal:
                usable[t] &= ~(Z[t] >= exclude_z)                       # an anomaly never becomes the baseline
    if win_k > 1:                                                       # WINDOW EVIDENCE: median of the last win_k ticks, judged with
        for j in range(F):                                              # the spread of a median of n values (spread x 1.25 / sqrt(n)):

                continue

    sym = Z[:, :, sym_i].max(axis=2) if sym_i else np.zeros((T, S))
    return Scores(Z, MED, SC, own, sym, np.maximum(own, sym), J.any(axis=2), common, feats)


def normalise(sc, ref, q=0.995, features=None):
    """Per-series noise normalisation: a series that is jumpy even when HEALTHY (ref = scores on healthy history) is shrunk so its
    healthy extreme sits at z = 3; quiet series are never amplified. Then one global tau is fair to every service."""
    Zr = np.where(ref.z > 0, ref.z, np.nan)
    with warnings.catch_warnings():
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", RuntimeWarning)
        Q = np.nan_to_num(np.nanquantile(Zr, q, axis=0), nan=0.0)          # (S, F) healthy extreme per series
    Z = sc.z / np.maximum(Q / 3.0, 1.0)
    fs = sc.features or features
    own_i = [i for i, f in enumerate(fs) if SPEC[f][2] == "own"]; sym_i = [i for i, f in enumerate(fs) if SPEC[f][2] == "sym"]
    own = Z[:, :, own_i].max(axis=2); sym = Z[:, :, sym_i].max(axis=2)
    return Scores(Z, sc.med, sc.scale, own, sym, np.maximum(own, sym), sc.judged, sc.common, fs)

"""STEP 3 THRESHOLD + PERSISTENCE: tau is calibrated on healthy history to a false-alarm budget (alarms/hour).

import numpy as np

def persist(raw, judged, k):
    if k <= 1:
        return raw.copy()
    run = np.zeros(raw.shape, int)
    for t in range(raw.shape[0]):
        prev = run[t - 1] if t else 0
        run[t] = np.where(raw[t], prev + 1, np.where(judged[t], 0, prev))   # unknown ticks keep the streak
    fl = run >= k
    for t in range(raw.shape[0] - 2, -1, -1):
        fl[t] |= raw[t] & fl[t + 1]                                            # mark the whole streak, not only its end
    return fl


def flags_for(sc, tau, persistence=2):
    return persist(sc.zs >= tau, sc.judged, persistence)


def episodes_per_hour(fl, step):
    starts = (fl[1:] & ~fl[:-1]).sum() + fl[0].sum()
    return starts / max(fl.shape[0] * step / 3600, 1e-9)


def calibrate(sc, step, budget=2.0, persistence=2, grid=np.arange(1.5, 40.0, 0.25)):
    for tau in grid:
        if episodes_per_hour(flags_for(sc, tau, persistence), step) <= budget:

"""STEP 6 RISK (cascading-failure prediction): who is pulled in next, and how likely.

def risk(p, sc, fl, t, tau, edge_prob=None):
    idx = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(p.services)}; out = {}
    for c, e in p.edges:
        if fl[t, idx[e]] and not fl[t, idx[c]]:
            sev = min(1.0, sc.zs[t, idx[e]] / (2 * tau))
            out[c] = 1 - (1 - out.get(c, 0.0)) * (1 - sev * (edge_prob or {}).get((c, e), 0.5))
    return pd.Series(out, dtype=float).sort_values(ascending=False)


class EdgeLearner:
    """Edge propagation probabilities learned ACROSS incidents from detector flags only (empirical-Bayes prior)."""

    def __init__(self, max_lag: int = 4, strength: float = 2.0):
        self.max_lag, self.strength = max_lag, strength
        self.n_e, self.n_both = {}, {}

    def update(self, services, edges, flags, t0=0, t1=None):
        idx = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(services)}
        t1 = flags.shape[0] if t1 is None else t1
        for caller, callee in edges:
            if caller not in idx or callee not in idx:
                continue
            ci, ei = idx[caller], idx[callee]
            key = (callee, caller)
            for t in range(t0, t1):
                if flags[t, ei] and (t == 0 or not flags[t - 1, ei]):
                    self.n_e[key] = self.n_e.get(key, 0) + 1
                    self.n_both[key] = self.n_both.get(key, 0) + int(flags[t:min(t + self.max_lag + 1, t1), ci].any())

    def probs(self, edges):
        tot_e, tot_b = sum(self.n_e.values()), sum(self.n_both.values())
        p0 = (tot_b + 0.5) / (tot_e + 1.0) if tot_e else 0.5
        return {(callee, caller): (self.n_both.get((callee, caller), 0) + self.strength * p0) /
                (self.n_e.get((callee, caller), 0) + self.strength) for caller, callee in edges}


def path_probabilities(services, probs, max_hops: int = 3) -> np.ndarray:
    """P[u, v] = best (max-product) probability that a failure at u reaches v within max_hops (influence callee -> caller)."""
    n = len(services)
    idx = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(services)}
    adj = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    for (u, v), p in probs.items():
        if u in idx and v in idx:
            adj[idx[u]].append((idx[v], p))
    P = np.zeros((n, n))
    for src in range(n):
        best = {src: (1.0, 0)}
        heap = [(-1.0, src, 0)]
        while heap:
            negp, u, hops = heapq.heappop(heap)
            if -negp < best[u][0] or hops == max_hops:
                continue
            for v, p in adj[u]:
                cand = -negp * p
                if v not in best or cand > best[v][0]:
                    best[v] = (cand, hops + 1)
                    heapq.heappush(heap, (-cand, v, hops + 1))
        for v, (p, _) in best.items():
            if v != src:
                P[src, v] = p
    return P


def cascade_risk(a_now: np.ndarray, P: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """risk[v] = 1 - prod_u (1 - a_u * P[u, v])   (noisy-OR over every upstream cause)."""
    contrib = 1.0 - a_now[:, None] * P
    np.fill_diagonal(contrib, 1.0)
    return 1.0 - contrib.prod(axis=0)

"""Baselines the method is compared against. Each takes the same inputs as the method and returns services best-first.

Already in the bench (bench.rank_window): most_abnormal (highest score), earliest (first flagged), random, original
(the user's original notebook: whole-dataset z > 3, rank by count). Added here, re-implemented from their papers'
descriptions (NOT the authors' code, so call them "-style"):
  * nsigma      (RCAEval's N-Sigma): per metric, |x - mean| / std of the pre-fault period, rank by the max over the window
  * baro        (BARO, Pham et al. FSE'24, RobustScorer part): |x - median| / IQR of the pre-fault period, rank by the max
  * pagerank    (MicroRCA-style): personalised PageRank on the call graph, edges toward dependencies weighted by anomaly
These three are given the true fault start (a), as in the published benchmarks; our method only gets the window.
"""
import warnings

import numpy as np

PRE = 60  # ticks of pre-fault history the window baselines use (= our baseline window)


def _pre_post(p, a, b):
    X = p.X.copy()
    pos = np.nanmin(np.where(np.isnan(X), np.inf, X), axis=(0, 1)) >= 0
    X[:, :, pos] = np.log1p(np.maximum(X[:, :, pos], 0))
    return X[max(0, a - PRE):a], X[a:b]


def nsigma(p, sc, fl, a, b):
    pre, post = _pre_post(p, a, b)
    with warnings.catch_warnings():
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", RuntimeWarning)
        mu, sd = np.nanmean(pre, axis=0), np.nanstd(pre, axis=0)
        z = np.abs(post - mu) / np.where(sd > 1e-9, sd, np.nan)
        s = np.nan_to_num(np.nanmax(z, axis=(0, 2)), nan=0.0)
    return [p.services[i] for i in np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")]


def baro(p, sc, fl, a, b):
    pre, post = _pre_post(p, a, b)
    with warnings.catch_warnings():
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", RuntimeWarning)
        med = np.nanmedian(pre, axis=0)
        iqr = np.nanpercentile(pre, 75, axis=0) - np.nanpercentile(pre, 25, axis=0)
        z = np.abs(post - med) / np.where(iqr > 1e-9, iqr, np.nan)
        s = np.nan_to_num(np.nanmax(z, axis=(0, 2)), nan=0.0)
    return [p.services[i] for i in np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")]


def pagerank(p, sc, fl, a, b, damping=0.85, iters=100):
    """Personalised PageRank, call edges reversed toward dependencies, weights = callee anomaly, teleport = anomaly."""
    n = len(p.services)
    s = sc.zs[a:b].max(axis=0)
    an = s / (s.max() or 1.0)
    idx = {x: i for i, x in enumerate(p.services)}
    W = np.zeros((n, n))
    for c, e in p.edges:
        if c in idx and e in idx:
            W[idx[c], idx[e]] = an[idx[e]] + 1e-3
    W += np.diag(an * 0.1)
    rows = W.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    T = np.divide(W, rows, out=np.zeros_like(W), where=rows > 0)
    tele = an / an.sum() if an.sum() else np.full(n, 1.0 / n)
    r = tele.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        r = (1 - damping) * tele + damping * (r @ T + r * (rows.ravel() == 0))
    return [p.services[i] for i in np.argsort(-r, kind="stable")]

"""Statistical tests used to judge the RCA and cascade methods.

Which test for which question, and why (the short version; the report has the long one):

    * A@1 is 0/1 per fault           -> exact McNemar test (only discordant pairs carry information)
    * reciprocal rank (RR) per fault -> Wilcoxon signed-rank (no normality needed) + paired t-test (asked for; shown with a
                                        Shapiro-Wilk check of the differences, which usually says normality does NOT hold)
    * effect sizes                   -> matched-pairs rank-biserial r (Wilcoxon), Cohen's dz (t), risk difference (McNemar)

    d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return (c - h, c + h)


def cluster_bootstrap(values: np.ndarray, clusters: np.ndarray, stat=np.mean, n_boot: int = 4000, seed: int = 0):
    """Resample whole clusters (seeds) with replacement, then pool. Returns (estimate, lo, hi)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    values, clusters = np.asarray(values, float), np.asarray(clusters)
    ids = np.unique(clusters)
    groups = [values[clusters == c] for c in ids]
    boots = np.empty(n_boot)
    for b in range(n_boot):
        pick = rng.integers(0, len(ids), len(ids))
        boots[b] = stat(np.concatenate([groups[i] for i in pick]))
    return float(stat(values)), float(np.nanpercentile(boots, 2.5)), float(np.nanpercentile(boots, 97.5))


def block_bootstrap_rate(events: np.ndarray, step_s: float, block: int | None = None, n_boot: int = 4000, seed: int = 0):
    """Moving-block bootstrap CI of an event rate (per hour) on a 0/1 series of event onsets. Blocks keep bursts together."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = np.asarray(events, float)
    T = len(x)
    block = block or max(1, int(round(T ** (1 / 3))))
    nb = int(math.ceil(T / block))
    starts = np.arange(0, T - block + 1)
    hours = T * step_s / 3600
    boots = np.empty(n_boot)
    for b in range(n_boot):
        s = rng.choice(starts, nb)
        boots[b] = np.concatenate([x[i:i + block] for i in s])[:T].sum() / hours
    return float(x.sum() / hours), float(np.percentile(boots, 2.5)), float(np.percentile(boots, 97.5)), block

def mcnemar_exact(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray):
    """a, b: 0/1 outcomes of two methods on the same items. Exact binomial test on the discordant pairs."""
    a, b = np.asarray(a, bool), np.asarray(b, bool)
    n01, n10 = int((~a & b).sum()), int((a & ~b).sum())
    n = n01 + n10
    p = 1.0 if n == 0 else float(stats.binomtest(n10, n, 0.5).pvalue)
    return dict(a_only=n10, b_only=n01, p=p, risk_diff=float(a.mean() - b.mean()))


def paired_numeric(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, n_perm: int = 20000, seed: int = 0):
    """Wilcoxon signed-rank, paired t-test, Shapiro-Wilk on the differences, and a sign-flip permutation test."""
    a, b = np.asarray(a, float), np.asarray(b, float)
    d = a - b
    out = dict(mean_diff=float(d.mean()), median_diff=float(np.median(d)), n=int(len(d)), n_nonzero=int((d != 0).sum()))
    if out["n_nonzero"] == 0:
        return {**out, "wilcoxon_p": 1.0, "rank_biserial": 0.0, "t": 0.0, "t_p": 1.0, "cohen_dz": 0.0,
                "shapiro_p": float("nan"), "perm_p": 1.0}
    w = stats.wilcoxon(a, b, zero_method="wilcox")
    nz = d[d != 0]
    r = stats.rankdata(np.abs(nz))
    rb = float((r[nz > 0].sum() - r[nz < 0].sum()) / r.sum())          # matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation
    t = stats.ttest_rel(a, b)
    sd = d.std(ddof=1)
    sh = stats.shapiro(d[:5000]).pvalue if len(d) >= 3 and sd > 0 else float("nan")
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    obs = abs(d.mean())
    flips = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(n_perm, len(d)))
    perm_p = float(((np.abs((flips * d).mean(axis=1)) >= obs - 1e-12).sum() + 1) / (n_perm + 1))
    return {**out, "wilcoxon_p": float(w.pvalue), "rank_biserial": rb, "t": float(t.statistic), "t_p": float(t.pvalue),
            "cohen_dz": float(d.mean() / sd) if sd > 0 else 0.0, "shapiro_p": float(sh), "perm_p": perm_p}


def cluster_signflip(a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, clusters: np.ndarray, n_perm: int = 20000, seed: int = 0):
    """Permutation test that respects dependence: average the paired difference within each cluster (seed), then flip the
    sign of whole clusters. Exact (all 2^k flips) when k <= 16."""
    a, b, clusters = np.asarray(a, float), np.asarray(b, float), np.asarray(clusters)
    ids = np.unique(clusters)
    m = np.array([(a[clusters == c] - b[clusters == c]).mean() for c in ids])
    obs = abs(m.mean())
    if len(ids) <= 16:                                   # exact: every one of the 2^k sign patterns
        signs = np.array(list(product([-1.0, 1.0], repeat=len(ids))))
        p = float((np.abs((signs * m).mean(axis=1)) >= obs - 1e-12).mean())
    else:                                                # Monte Carlo; (count + 1) / (n + 1) so p is never reported as 0
        signs = np.random.default_rng(seed).choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(n_perm, len(ids)))
        p = float(((np.abs((signs * m).mean(axis=1)) >= obs - 1e-12).sum() + 1) / (n_perm + 1))
    return dict(k_clusters=int(len(ids)), mean_cluster_diff=float(m.mean()), p=p,
                clusters_better=int((m > 0).sum()), clusters_worse=int((m < 0).sum()))


def holm(pvals: dict) -> dict:
    keys = sorted(pvals, key=lambda k: pvals[k])
    out, running = {}, 0.0
    for i, k in enumerate(keys):
        running = max(running, min(1.0, pvals[k] * (len(keys) - i)))
        out[k] = running
    return out

def friedman_nemenyi(M: np.ndarray, names: list[str], alpha: float = 0.05):
    """M: (items, methods), higher = better. Friedman chi2, Kendall's W, mean ranks (1 = best) and the Nemenyi CD."""
    n, k = M.shape
    ranks = np.vstack([stats.rankdata(-row) for row in M])             # 1 = best, ties averaged
    chi2, p = stats.friedmanchisquare(*[M[:, j] for j in range(k)])
    W = chi2 / (n * (k - 1))
    q = stats.studentized_range.ppf(1 - alpha, k, np.inf) / math.sqrt(2)
    cd = q * math.sqrt(k * (k + 1) / (6 * n))
    mr = ranks.mean(axis=0)
    return dict(chi2=float(chi2), p=float(p), kendall_w=float(W), cd=float(cd),
                mean_rank={nm: float(r) for nm, r in zip(names, mr)})


def cochran_q(B: np.ndarray):
    """B: (items, methods) 0/1. Cochran's Q test that all methods have the same success rate."""
    B = np.asarray(B, float)
    k = B.shape[1]
    col, row = B.sum(axis=0), B.sum(axis=1)
    N = B.sum()
    den = k * N - (row ** 2).sum()
    if den == 0:
        return dict(Q=0.0, p=1.0)
    Q = (k - 1) * (k * (col ** 2).sum() - N ** 2) / den
    return dict(Q=float(Q), p=float(stats.chi2.sf(Q, k - 1)))

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- time series

def autocorr(x: np.ndarray, lags=(1, 2, 5, 10)):
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    x = x - x.mean()
    v = (x * x).sum()
    return {int(L): float((x[:-L] * x[L:]).sum() / v) if v > 0 and L < len(x) else float("nan") for L in lags}


def ljung_box(x: np.ndarray, lags: int = 10):
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    n = len(x)
    x = x - x.mean()
    v = (x * x).sum()
    if v == 0:
        return dict(Q=0.0, p=1.0, lags=lags)
    r = np.array([(x[:-k] * x[k:]).sum() / v for k in range(1, lags + 1)])
    Q = n * (n + 2) * np.sum(r ** 2 / (n - np.arange(1, lags + 1)))
    return dict(Q=float(Q), p=float(stats.chi2.sf(Q, lags)), lags=lags)


def poisson_rate_test(k1: int, t1: float, k2: int, t2: float):
    """Exact conditional test of equal Poisson rates (k events in exposure t). Returns rate ratio and two-sided p."""
    n = k1 + k2
    if n == 0:
        return dict(rate_ratio=float("nan"), p=1.0)
    p = float(stats.binomtest(k1, n, t1 / (t1 + t2)).pvalue)
    rr = (k1 / t1) / (k2 / t2) if k2 > 0 else float("inf")
    return dict(rate_ratio=float(rr), p=p)


def dispersion_index(events: np.ndarray, block: int):
    """Variance/mean of event counts in consecutive blocks. ~1 = Poisson; >1 = bursty (overdispersed)."""
    x = np.asarray(events, float)
    nb = len(x) // block
    c = x[:nb * block].reshape(nb, block).sum(axis=1)
    return float(c.var(ddof=1) / c.mean()) if nb > 1 and c.mean() > 0 else float("nan")


def newey_west_var(d: np.ndarray, lag: int | None = None):
    d = np.asarray(d, float)
    n = len(d)
    lag = lag if lag is not None else int(math.floor(4 * (n / 100) ** (2 / 9)))
    u = d - d.mean()
    s = (u * u).sum() / n
    for L in range(1, lag + 1):
        w = 1 - L / (lag + 1)
        s += 2 * w * (u[:-L] * u[L:]).sum() / n
    return s / n, lag


def diebold_mariano(loss_a: np.ndarray, loss_b: np.ndarray, lag: int | None = None):
    """H0: equal expected loss over time. Positive statistic = A has the larger loss. HAC (Newey-West) variance."""
    d = np.asarray(loss_a, float) - np.asarray(loss_b, float)
    var, lag = newey_west_var(d, lag)
    if var <= 0:
        return dict(dm=0.0, p=1.0, mean_loss_diff=float(d.mean()), lag=lag)
    dm = d.mean() / math.sqrt(var)
    return dict(dm=float(dm), p=float(2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(dm))), mean_loss_diff=float(d.mean()), lag=lag)


def kaplan_meier(time: np.ndarray, event: np.ndarray):
    """Returns (times, survival) step function and the median (None if survival never drops below 0.5)."""
    time, event = np.asarray(time, float), np.asarray(event, bool)
    order = np.argsort(time)
    time, event = time[order], event[order]
    uniq = np.unique(time[event])
    S, out_t, out_s = 1.0, [0.0], [1.0]
    for t in uniq:
        at_risk = (time >= t).sum()
        d = ((time == t) & event).sum()
        S *= 1 - d / at_risk
        out_t.append(float(t)); out_s.append(float(S))
    med = next((t for t, s in zip(out_t, out_s) if s <= 0.5), None)
    return out_t, out_s, med


def logrank(time_a, event_a, time_b, event_b):
    ta, ea, tb, eb = map(np.asarray, (time_a, event_a, time_b, event_b))
    ea, eb = ea.astype(bool), eb.astype(bool)
    times = np.unique(np.concatenate([ta[ea], tb[eb]]))
    O_E, V = 0.0, 0.0
    for t in times:
        na, nb = (ta >= t).sum(), (tb >= t).sum()
        da, db = ((ta == t) & ea).sum(), ((tb == t) & eb).sum()
        n, d = na + nb, da + db
        if n < 2:
            continue
        O_E += da - d * na / n
        V += d * (na / n) * (1 - na / n) * (n - d) / (n - 1)
    if V <= 0:
        return dict(chi2=0.0, p=1.0)
    chi2 = O_E ** 2 / V
    return dict(chi2=float(chi2), p=float(stats.chi2.sf(chi2, 1)), observed_minus_expected_a=float(O_E))


def mann_kendall(y: np.ndarray):
    y = np.asarray(y, float)
    n = len(y)
    s = sum(np.sign(y[j] - y[i]) for i in range(n - 1) for j in range(i + 1, n))
    var = n * (n - 1) * (2 * n + 5) / 18
    z = 0.0 if s == 0 else (s - np.sign(s)) / math.sqrt(var)
    return dict(S=float(s), z=float(z), p=float(2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))), n=n)


def cusum(y: np.ndarray, target: float | None = None):
    y = np.asarray(y, float)
    target = float(np.mean(y)) if target is None else target
    return np.cumsum(y - target).tolist()

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- probabilistic predictions

def roc_auc(scores: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray):
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    if y.all() or (~y).all():
        return float("nan")
    r = stats.rankdata(s)
    n1, n0 = y.sum(), (~y).sum()
    return float((r[y].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0))


def average_precision(scores: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray):
    s, y = np.asarray(scores, float), np.asarray(labels, bool)
    if not y.any():
        return float("nan")
    order = np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")
    y = y[order]
    hits = np.cumsum(y)
    return float((hits[y] / (np.flatnonzero(y) + 1)).mean())


def brier(prob: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray):
    return float(np.mean((np.asarray(prob, float) - np.asarray(labels, float)) ** 2))


def ece(prob: np.ndarray, labels: np.ndarray, bins: int = 10):
    p, y = np.asarray(prob, float), np.asarray(labels, float)
    edges = np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)
    tot, rows = 0.0, []
    for i in range(bins):
        m = (p >= edges[i]) & (p < edges[i + 1] if i < bins - 1 else p <= edges[i + 1])
        if m.any():
            tot += m.mean() * abs(p[m].mean() - y[m].mean())
            rows.append(dict(bin=f"{edges[i]:.1f}-{edges[i+1]:.1f}", n=int(m.sum()), predicted=float(p[m].mean()),
                             observed=float(y[m].mean())))
    return float(tot), rows

5. Which statistics, and why

A t-test assumes independent observations from one fixed distribution, with roughly normal differences. Three properties of this data break that assumption, so each question gets the test whose assumptions actually hold.

  • Paired: the same faults are scored by every method, so independent-sample tests would waste the pairing.
  • Discrete and skewed: A@1 is 0/1, and reciprocal rank takes a few values. Shapiro–Wilk on the paired differences gives p ≈ 3e-26, so the differences are clearly not normal.
  • Dependent and changing over time: all seeds re-use the same 77 minutes of background, so faults within a seed share a timeline. The healthy anomaly score has lag-1 autocorrelation 0.56 (lag 10: 0.26), and Ljung–Box gives Q = 2029 with p < 0.001. Alarms come in bursts (dispersion index 2.87, where 1 would be Poisson).

Claims the evidence supports:

  • A label-free detector whose false-alarm rate is chosen and then measured: 0.15/h on held-out seeds, 0.08–0.24 with a burst-aware CI.
  • The silence signal and per-series normalisation, each with a large, significant effect: +25 and +9 points of A@1, p < 0.001 under every test.
  • Significantly better than PageRank (MicroRCA-style), N-Sigma, BARO-style and whole-dataset z-scores on this bench.

  • That explain-away attribution or precedence beats "most abnormal". It does not on this bench.
  • Any cascade-prediction accuracy from the bench, because of the circularity.
  • Numbers from other papers as directly comparable. Their data is labelled and pre-processed; this data is live and replayed.
  • Good performance on slow faults.

14. Limits and next steps

  • One background recording. Every bench seed replays the same 77 minutes. The seed-level tests account for this, but the results describe this application. Next: record hours of healthy traffic on ShopFlow, LedgerLine and DeathStar, and repeat.
  • Injected faults encode assumptions (section 4), so real faults are needed. The benches' lab/chaos.sh (stop, pause, cpu, net, crash, flap) and lab/cascade.sh write a ledger and give independently observed spread. That fixes the cascade circularity above.
  • Slow faults. Try per-request traces (self time from spans rather than 15 s aggregates), longer windows, or the window-evidence switch on slow faults only.
  • Baselines are re-implementations from paper descriptions ("-style"). Run the authors' code (RCAEval) on the same data before claiming a comparison with the published versions.

Part C · Tough questions from a judge, with honest answers

41 questions a sceptical examiner might ask, each answered from the evidence above. Where the honest answer is "we have not tested that" or "that is a weakness", it says so. Click a question to open the answer.

About the data

Your faults are fake. Why should I believe any of this?
Half fair. The background traffic is real: 77 minutes recorded from a working shop, with all its natural noise, bursts and gaps. Only the faults are simulated, and every assumption about how a fault looks is written down (section 4). On top of that there is one real fault (a container stopped on AWS), where the true root ranked #1 of 27. One real case is a demonstration, not proof. The next step is real faults on the test systems we built (stop, pause, CPU starvation, network cut), which give real data with a written ledger.
You replay the same 77 minutes for 10 hours. Isn't that the same data over and over?
Yes, and we say so. That is exactly why the statistics treat each seed as one unit (seed-level permutation test, cluster bootstrap) instead of pretending all 751 faults are independent. It also means the results describe this application's traffic; a longer recording from more systems is needed before generalising.
The OpenTelemetry Demo is a toy. Real systems have hundreds of services.
It is the reference application the monitoring community uses to test tools, with real tracing across several programming languages, but it has 17 services, not 500. This version has not been tested at hundreds of services. Two things would get harder: more noisy services competing for the top spot, and a system-wide false-alarm budget pushing the threshold up (fewer alarms per service). We would expect accuracy to drop at that scale until it is measured.
You only inject faults into 11 of the 17 services. Isn't that cherry-picking?
The rule was fixed before any results: a service must report data in at least 40% of ticks. The other six are idle most of the time (no requests, so no measurements), and a fault in a service nobody is calling is invisible to any trace-based method. The honest scope is: results apply to services that carry traffic.
15-second measurements are very coarse. Real tools look at every request.
Agreed, and it is the most likely reason slow faults fail (16%). Per-request traces would give each service's own time far more precisely. It is listed as the first thing to try.
What about a database, the network, or shared infrastructure failing?
Not in this test. One risk is real: common-mode removal subtracts what many services do together, so a fault in something everyone depends on could partly be subtracted away. That case needs its own test (the benches can cut the network or starve a database).

About the method

Why not just use machine learning / deep learning?
Those models need many labelled past incidents to learn from, which most teams do not have, and they must be retrained every time the system changes. This method needs no labels and explains every decision ('error rate 20× its own normal'). If a team does have labelled incidents, a trained model might beat it. That comparison has not been run here, so we do not claim otherwise.
Why median and MAD instead of the usual average and standard deviation?
One huge spike drags the average and inflates the standard deviation, which hides real faults and causes false ones. The original notebook did exactly that (whole-dataset average and standard deviation): 45% accuracy and 80 false alarms per hour, against 70% and 0.15 per hour here.
Why a 15-minute baseline window? Why not 5 minutes or an hour?
It was tested: windows of 10, 15 and 25 minutes were tried on the tuning seeds. The best (25 minutes, with other settings) reached 70.8% on the judging seeds against 69.5% for the default, a difference that is not significant (p = 1.00). So the window is not a fragile magic number; the result is stable across it.
Your threshold of 3.25 looks like a magic number.
It is not chosen by hand. You choose a false-alarm budget (default: at most 2 per hour), and the method picks the lowest threshold that stays within it on healthy data. Change the budget and the threshold changes; the trade-off is shown in section 8.
What happens when the system legitimately changes, for example after a deploy or when traffic grows?
The baseline follows the data: after about 15 minutes the new level is normal. Known weakness: to stop long faults becoming 'normal', ticks that were themselves strongly anomalous are left out of the baseline. A permanent legitimate jump that is large enough would therefore keep alarming until someone resets it. That needs a fix: an acknowledge button, or a limit on how long a level can be excluded.
Black Friday: traffic triples. Does everything go red?
That is what common-mode removal is for: if more than half the services jump together on the same signal, the shared part is subtracted. On the real DeathStar recording, network traffic jumped on almost every container at once and it handled it. The 10-hour test has no such bursts, so the ablation shows no measurable effect there; it is not proven at scale.
What if the broken service has no monitoring at all?
If a service sends nothing while the services calling it show unexplained errors, it is inferred as the likely culprit ('blind-spot inference'). And a service that normally reports and suddenly goes quiet is caught by the silence signal: that is why hangs are found 100% of the time.
What if two services break at the same time?
Not tested: every test fault has one root. The ranking would probably put both near the top, but A@1 assumes a single answer. Multi-root faults need their own test and metric.
Your own table shows the 'explain-away' step adds nothing. Why keep it?
Correct: on the test, picking the most abnormal service does as well (70.6% against 69.5%, p = 1.00). We keep it as an option because it is cheap and it makes the explanation ('this one's errors are caused by that one'), but we do not claim it improves accuracy. The real contribution is the scoring: silence, noise normalisation and a calibrated threshold.
Why does it fail on slow faults?
When a service gets slow, everything that calls it gets slow too, because they wait. The only clean way to tell the source from the waiters is each service's own processing time, and at 15-second resolution that signal is noisy, so the slow root often does not even cross the alarm line. That explanation is our best hypothesis, not a proven finding. The next things to try are per-request traces and longer windows for slow-type signals.
The method needs the call graph. What if it is wrong or missing?
The graph comes automatically from traces, so it reflects real calls. On the real crash, removing the graph entirely still ranked the root #1. On the test, the graph matters mostly for the cascade prediction, because ranking without explain-away is just as accurate.
So isn't your method just 'pick the most abnormal service'?
For the final step, on this test, yes, and the report says so. What makes 'most abnormal' work is how 'abnormal' is measured: without the silence signal it falls to 45%, and without noise normalisation to 60% with eight times the false alarms.
How fast is it? Can it run live?
Scoring a whole 10-hour run (2,456 ticks × 17 services) takes about one and a half seconds on a laptop, so live use every 15 seconds is easy. The cost grows with the number of services times the window length.
Doesn't requiring two ticks in a row delay every alarm by 30 seconds?
When the root's own alarm fires, it typically fires in the same tick the fault starts: the median delay is 0 s, and the mean is 7 s. The confirmation looks back and marks the streak from its first tick. Persistence 1 raises false alarms about tenfold for a small gain in detections (section 10).
What happens if the monitoring itself breaks, for example the collector goes down?
Honest answer: probably an alarm storm. Every service would go silent at once and the silence signal would fire everywhere. The crash signal already guards against this ('down' is only declared if other services are still reporting), but silence is not guarded yet. Needed fix: if (nearly) everything goes silent together, report 'monitoring outage' instead of blaming services.

About the statistics

Why not just do a t-test like everyone else?
A t-test assumes independent measurements and bell-shaped differences. Here the differences are mostly exactly 0 or ±1 (not bell-shaped), faults share background traffic (not independent), and neighbouring ticks are strongly related (time series). We ran the t-test anyway because it is familiar, and it agrees with the others. But we rely on tests whose assumptions hold: McNemar, Wilcoxon, a seed-level permutation test, block bootstrap and Diebold–Mariano.
With 751 faults everything is 'significant'. So what?
Right, which is why effect sizes are reported next to every p-value. The differences are large, not just significant: 70% against PageRank's 42%. Ours is right and PageRank wrong in 231 faults; the reverse happens in only 21. And where a difference is tiny (tuned vs default, explain-away vs most abnormal), the report says 'no measurable difference'.
Your faults are not independent, so your p-values are too optimistic.
That is exactly why there is a seed-level test: it averages within each of the 20 judging seeds and treats each seed as one unit. Ours beat PageRank in 20 of 20 seeds. The chance of that happening if they were equal is about one in a million.
You tested 22 variants. Some will look significant by pure chance.
Every family of p-values is Holm-corrected, which raises the bar in proportion to the number of tests. The big effects survive easily; the small ones are reported as not significant.
Did you tune your method on the test data?
No. Seeds 0–2 were used for development and for the settings search; seeds 3–22 were only used for the final numbers. Accuracy is 70% on the development seeds and 70% on the judging seeds (no difference, p = 0.96), which is what you expect when nothing was fitted to the test.
Your earlier documents said 75%. Now it is 70%. Which is it?
The 75% came from only 3 judging seeds (113 faults). With 20 seeds (751 faults) the estimate is 69.5%, with a 95% range of 66–73%. The bigger sample is the one to quote.
Why should I trust baselines you re-implemented yourselves?
You should be cautious. They are written from the papers' descriptions ('-style'), not the authors' code, and a weaker re-implementation would flatter us. We reduce the risk by also comparing only on slow and error faults, where every method can see the root. The proper fix, running the authors' own code (RCAEval) on the same data, is listed as a next step.
N-Sigma gets 9%. Did you sabotage it?
It is blind to crashes and hangs by design: it scores measurement values, and a crashed or frozen service has none, so it scores about 1% there. On slow and error faults it does better but still struggles, because very quiet measurements have a tiny spread and a small wobble looks enormous. That is a real property of plain N-Sigma, which our noise normalisation exists to fix. It is still a re-implementation, so treat the exact number with care.
Diebold–Mariano is an economics test. Why is it here?
It was designed to compare two forecasters over time when errors in neighbouring periods are related, which is exactly the situation of two detectors judged tick by tick. The maths does not care whether the series is inflation or service alarms.
What is 'censoring' and why does it matter?
Some faults are never detected. Averaging only the detected ones would make a detector that misses hard faults look fast. Censoring counts a missed fault as 'not detected by the end' instead of throwing it away, so slow or missing detection is penalised honestly.
Is 0.15 false alarms per hour actually good?
It is about one false alarm every 7 hours for the whole system, with a 95% range of 0.08–0.24 per hour, well under the 2-per-hour budget it was set to. Whether that is acceptable depends on the team. It was measured on the test traffic, not on months of production.

About cascade prediction

Your cascade prediction scores 0.995. That is too good to be true.
It is, and the report says so plainly. The test spreads faults along the same call graph the predictor walks, so even '1 ÷ number of hops' gets the same score. That number measures how the test was built, not the predictor, and we do not claim it.
So does cascade prediction work at all?
Modestly. The one fair number is the live risk score: at the root's first alarm, among services not yet alarming, it ranks the ones that really get pulled in above the others with AUC 0.68 (0.5 = guessing). Useful as a 'watch these next' list, not as precise probabilities.
Why not learn the propagation probabilities from data?
We did, without labels: count how often a caller alarms soon after its callee alarms. It did not beat the simple 0.5 prior (p = 0.18), and its probabilities were less honest (calibration error 0.17 against 0.12).
Why noisy-OR? It assumes causes are independent.
It is the simplest reasonable model and it is easy to explain. The independence assumption is probably wrong when two broken dependencies share a cause. With this little evidence that cascade prediction works at all, a more complex model would not be justified yet.

About real-world use and novelty

Has it ever worked on a real system?
On one real crash, yes: post-storage-service-1 stopped for 124 s on AWS, ranked #1 of 27, with no false alarm before it. In an earlier live test where a service was frozen (not stopped), it detected the freeze but did not pinpoint the service (rank 7–8 of 27) from container statistics alone. Both are reported.
Why would an engineer use this instead of a commercial monitoring product?
No comparison with commercial products has been done, so we make no claim of being better. What this offers is different: no labels or training, every decision explained in plain terms, a false-alarm rate you choose and that is then measured, and an open, testable method.
What is actually new here?
Not the idea of ranking by anomaly or graph propagation; those exist, and our tests did not support novelty claims for precedence or blast radius. The defensible contributions are three. First, a label-free detector whose false-alarm rate is chosen and then measured on held-out data. Second, the silence and hard-evidence signals, each with a large measured effect. Third, an evaluation that is honest about dependence, tuning and failure cases.
Give me the situations where your method fails.
  • Slow faults (16%).
  • Error faults where a noisy caller outscores the root (61%).
  • A permanent legitimate change that keeps alarming.
  • The monitoring collector going down (alarm storm).
  • Several services failing at once (untested).
  • A shared dependency hitting everyone at once (common-mode may subtract it).
  • Services with too little traffic to measure.
  • Systems of hundreds of services (untested).
If you had one more month, what would you do?
Run real injected faults (stop, pause, CPU, network) on the two test systems with long healthy recordings. Run the authors' code for the baselines. Attack slow faults with per-request traces. Add the two safety fixes: monitoring-outage detection and a reset for permanent level changes.
